# Minto Pyramid

Busy readers stop after the first lines, so the answer goes there:
**conclusion → three reasons → evidence under each reason.** Drafts usually run
the other way - background, analysis, then the point at the bottom - because
that is the order the writer thought in. The job is to reverse the order
without changing what the author is saying.

## Two rules that outrank the structure

**Keep the author's voice.** This is a re-ordering, not a rewrite into
consultant-speak. Reuse their sentences and wording wherever they work; keep
names, numbers and dates exactly as written; keep their hedges - a "probably"
stays a "probably", because turning it into a certainty changes the claim. Add
only the connective words the new order needs.

**Never invent evidence.** Everything in the output must be traceable to the
draft or to something the user told you. When a reason has nothing under it,
write that plainly (`[no evidence in the draft]`) and tell the author what would
fill it. A visible gap is useful to them; a plausible-sounding filler is a
liability with their name on it. The same applies to the pyramid's shape: if
the draft only supports two real reasons, deliver two and say so rather than
padding a third.

## Picking the mode

| The user says something like | Mode |
|---|---|
| "fix this", "it rambles", "make this better", hands over a draft with no instruction | **2 then 1** - name the buried point, then restructure |
| "restructure", "tighten", "make it answer-first", "minto this" | **1 Restructure** |
| "what am I actually saying?", "what's my point?", "is the point clear?" | **2 Find the buried lede** |
| "are my reasons solid?", "does this argument hold up?", "poke holes in my case" | **3 Audit the reasons** |
| "cut the fluff", "too long but keep the structure", "what can go?" | **4 So-what pass** |
| "make it an email", "short version for my boss/exec", "they'll read it on their phone" | **5 Email version** |

If a request fits two rows, do the one that changes less and offer the other
in a line. Modes 2 and 3 are diagnoses: report, don't rewrite, unless asked.

## 1. Restructure

Rewrite the draft as:

1. **The conclusion, in one sentence, as the opening line.** It is what the author wants the reader to believe or do. If it needs an "and" to hold two unrelated things, it is two conclusions - pick the one the draft argues for and mention the other.
2. **Three reasons** that support it, each stated as a full claim ("The vendor's fix won't arrive before launch"), not a topic label ("Vendor").
3. **The evidence**, each piece under the reason it supports.

Cut what doesn't sit under one of the three. Then, after the rewrite, add a
short note listing what was cut and any evidence gaps, so the author can
object - nothing should disappear silently.

## 2. Find the buried lede

Report three things:

- **The conclusion the draft is building toward** - the sentence the author would say with ten seconds in a lift with their boss.
- **Where it is hiding.** Quote the line exactly as written and say how far down it sits (paragraph number, or "the last sentence of five paragraphs").
- If the draft never states it, say that, and offer the sentence, marked as your wording for them to confirm.

## 3. Audit the reasons

Test the author's reasons the way a sceptical reader would:

- **Overlap** - are any two the same point in different words? Name the pair and the single reason they collapse into.
- **Level** - is one a detail, feature or piece of evidence posing as a reason? Say which real reason it belongs under.
- **Coverage** - together, do they make the whole case? Name the obvious objection or question the draft leaves unanswered (cost, risk, "why now", "why not the alternative").

Return a verdict per reason, then a corrected set of three built from material
already in the draft. If the draft lacks a third, show the slot as open and
say what kind of reason would fill it - don't supply one from general
knowledge as if the author had argued it.

## 4. So-what pass

Go line by line. After each, ask "so what?" - what does this let the reader
conclude or do? Lines with no answer go. Show what survives as clean text,
then list what was deleted with a few words on why, so the author can rescue
anything that mattered for a reason you couldn't see.

## 5. Email version

Compress to an email with a body **under 150 words** in the same shape:

- **Subject line** states the conclusion or the ask.
- **First sentence** is the answer or the ask, with any deadline.
- **Three reasons**, one line each.
- **Evidence** only where a reason would not be believed without it - usually one number per reason at most.

Count the words of the body before delivering; if it is over, cut evidence
before cutting a reason. Keep the author's voice and sign-off.

## When not to force it

Some pieces shouldn't be answer-first: narrative writing, bad news to someone
who needs the context before the blow, and exploratory docs that have no
conclusion yet. Say so rather than bending the piece. For the exploratory
case, mode 2 is still useful - it tells the author they haven't decided.
